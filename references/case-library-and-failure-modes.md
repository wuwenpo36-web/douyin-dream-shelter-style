## Proven Disaster-To-Shelter Patterns

### Flash-Flood Bridge To Coconut Treehouse

The first frame is already first-person on a narrow wet bamboo bridge above a violent jungle flood. One hand grips the rope rail, both feet are visibly committed to the unstable route, and a large muddy surge is already entering the near field. Within about 1.5 seconds the water hits and fully covers the lens. The character remains briefly submerged, resurfaces with persistent droplets, raises and throws a coconut, and the coconut contact creates a second muddy splash occlusion. A tall, warmly lit treehouse shelter appears only as the splash clears; the character immediately moves through the flood, grabs the stair rail, and reaches the door.

Why it works: frame zero contains posture, fragile support, threat direction, scale, and time-to-impact without exposition. The sequence alternates anticipation -> impact -> disorientation -> reorientation -> agency -> wonder. It uses two causal full-frame occlusions and two audio-visual crescendos, while the elevated shelter visibly solves the flood rather than functioning as a decorative reveal.

Reusable lesson: begin retention-first action at the last safe second, let the hazard actually touch the body and lens, insert only a short comprehension gap before the object action, and keep moving toward entry after the reveal. Improve on the pattern by establishing the object source in frame zero or during the recovery instead of allowing it to appear without context.

### Waterfall Cliff To Pinecone Treehouse

An anchored worker climbs beside a huge waterfall in typhoon rain. A crosswind and slick hold rotate the body toward the water curtain. The camera passes through the waterfall, becomes briefly submerged and keeps droplets afterward. During the fall, the character throws a pinecone; brown woody mist occludes the frame; a giant treehouse appears below. The character lands on a pinecone-shaped cushion that compresses, settles, then rises toward the door.

Why it works: direct physical peril, a full-frame water occluder, a material-specific transformation, a themed safe landing, and a strong vertical scale reveal.

### Storm Sailboat To Drift-Bottle Airship

A small sailboat heels hard as a near-field wave approaches. The character throws a drift bottle; pale-blue condensation spreads; a refined bottle airship emerges low over the water. Its engines create spray and ripples. A ladder extends and physically contacts the boat before the character boards.

Why it works: the object belongs to the sea, the shelter solves isolation, and water interaction sells scale and realism.

### Coastal Tunnel Motorcycle To Flood Shelter

A rider in blue rain gear faces a storm surge entering a coastal tunnel. The leading water mass is already large inside the tunnel, with debris and spray approaching the lens. The shelter solution must address both water and loss of road—not merely appear outside.

Why it works: the threat fills the route and foreground rather than remaining a small wave beyond the tunnel mouth.

### Paris Winter Slide To Chocolate Palace

A first-person character glides on an elevated icy surface with the Eiffel Tower in snowy haze. Ice instability creates loss of control. A chocolate action creates a continuous brown slide leading into a warm Parisian palace shelter.

Why it works: the slide makes “silky” physical, the elevated route gives the shelter a logical lower endpoint, and the winter palette creates a strong warm/cold contrast.

### Ailao Mountain Courier To Mobile Delivery Shelter

A courier drives a loaded electric delivery tricycle past real Chinese mountain homes in fog and rain. The right edge is a mist-hidden steep ravine. Runoff, small stones, a falling tree, landslide, or road-edge failure escalates naturally. A cyan delivery box transforms through paper fibers and black tape geometry into a gigantic clean mobile shelter containing several rooms.

Why it works: occupation, cargo, route, object, and shelter identity are one system.

### Mango Floating Island

A cut mango or tray becomes a large rounded floating island with warm mango architecture, trees, garden, a protected pool or water feature, and multiple rooms. The interior uses cream, mango yellow, and leaf green with controlled storm views.

Why it works: the object supports a hospitable landscape, not just a literal fruit-shaped building.

### Pizza Airship And Herb Greenhouse

A pizza-inspired airship uses radial geometry, warm crust-like edges, refined stone and metal, and a real herb-supply greenhouse. The greenhouse grows basil, oregano, tomatoes, rosemary, peppers, and mushrooms, with protected skylight, storage, food, water, and power.

Why it works: the theme becomes a functioning food system rather than decorative toppings.

### Scorpion Airship

A black emperor scorpion becomes a refined segmented airship. Use black and charcoal as the base, then add burgundy, amber, brushed metal, or controlled RGB accents. Keep surfaces pristine and luxurious.

Why it works: it retains Scorpio's privacy, depth, and protection without becoming horror, funereal, or dilapidated.

## Evidence-Controlled Iteration Cases

These cases come from user-reviewed Seedance 2.5 iterations. Treat only the named mechanism as supported; do not upgrade the whole episode to a golden case.

### Reduced 15-Second Endpoint

A disaster-to-product-to-shelter sequence initially overloaded 15 seconds with vehicle danger, object retrieval, exiting, falling, product activation, smoke, giant reveal, recovery, and a final platform action. Moving the endpoint earlier produced a version the user described as broadly acceptable, with only local problems remaining.

Reusable lesson: in short one-take shelter videos, preserve the causal boundaries that viewers must read and remove the last destination or interaction before compressing turns, contact, falls, or reveal continuity. The success applies to action-budget reduction, not automatically to left/right vehicle logic, smoke continuity, or POV fidelity.

### Extremely Near, Extremely Large Shelter Reveal

“Very huge” alone produced a distant complete building. The scale problem was solved only after combining a near physical distance, 85–95% frame occupancy, mandatory cropping beyond the left/right/top/bottom edges, strict limits on grass and sky, and prohibitions on ultra-wide views, pulling back, rising, and aerial framing. The user explicitly confirmed the result was finally near and large enough.

Reusable lesson: a colossal shelter is a camera relationship, not an adjective. Lock distance, occupancy, allowed visible fragments, background limits, and forbidden camera escapes together. Evidence is limited to scale and proximity: the same result still missed architectural fidelity and clear brand text.

### Product Comfort Experience Chain

A pillow-led interior sequence linking tactile contact, comfortable sleep, and an exterior storm-to-morning change was described as close to the target overall. The brand experience direction worked, but the POV-to-third-person move remained too explicit and the time lapse was too weak.

Reusable lesson: a branded recovery ending can progress from touch -> material response -> body support -> protected sleep -> outside world changing while the interior remains stable. Do not mark the camera transition or time-lapse execution as proven until a later output is reviewed.

### Route Framework With Unresolved Embodied Motion

An exterior moving platform leading to a door and then to interior bounce pads was considered broadly usable apart from fake wind effects and stiff body motion. A later output showed abnormal feet and a seated-looking camera.

Reusable lesson: preserve the route concept, but treat moving-platform carriage, feet visibility, bounce physics, and hand timing as unresolved technical layers. Use the Seedance 2.5 embodied-motion method rather than copying the old timing literally.

## Exterior And Interior Patterns

- Giant pinecone or tree shelter: layered roof rhythm, substantial multi-level trunk architecture, smaller terrace, small door, one pinecone cushion.
- Drift-bottle gaming shelter: controlled windows, cork acoustic bedroom, focused RGB gaming room, drink cabinet, collectibles, and life-support facilities.
- Protected rainforest shelter: warm library hearth -> dry rain garden and bridge -> tree-canopy sleep room.
- Mango storm shelter: compact thematic elevator -> cave-like living and dining -> rain-sleep media suite -> protected terrace.
- Private membership refuge: warm star ceiling, food, rest, sleeping alcove, pet zone, charging, dry clothing, and supplies in one private suite.

## Brand And Atmosphere Patterns

- Show exterior danger through one moderate reinforced window rather than a glass wall.
- Let warm practical light spread through plaster, wood, textiles, food, and steam.
- Use a quiet ritual—hot drink, record, book, dog, meal, shower, charging—to make the release tangible.
- Let product or service appear at the exact moment it solves the pain point.
- Use exact product scale and packaging; a small item should remain small in hand or on a table.

## Recurring Failure Modes

### Disaster And Action

- Hazard does not cause the event, such as a water tanker exploding in an extreme-heat concept.
- Full catastrophe is already present in a “before” image.
- Threat stays distant or to the side and cannot interact with the character.
- Waterfall is beside the climber but the next action requires passing through it.
- Shelter appears before the object trigger.
- Lens droplets appear without spray, or disappear instantly.
- Fall or landing has no weight, compression, or momentum.

### POV And Anatomy

- Camera is too low, too high, crooked, or detached from the body.
- Arms are excessively long or duplicated.
- Hands and feet appear separated from the body.
- Too much body fills the frame and hides the environment.
- The prompt says first person but composes a third-person action poster.
- Feet, knees, or straight legs become a persistent foreground UI and turn standing POV into a seated ride.
- A platform moves away while the character and eye camera remain behind.
- Repeated bounce pads are shown moving toward the viewer instead of the body following an arc.
- Balancing hands remain symmetrically suspended and make the performance rigid.

### Shelter Exterior

- Building is too small because doors and windows are oversized.
- Theme is literal, toy-like, or cartoonish.
- Premium design is rendered dirty, broken, rusty, or randomly distressed.
- Terrace is huge and empty or functionally unsafe.
- Pure white background becomes a gray studio with a visible floor.
- Multi-view geometry changes between panels.
- A complete white-background architecture reference defeats the requested near-field crop; scale succeeds but local identity and brand text disappear.

### Interiors

- Huge glass facade removes the feeling of protection.
- Room is so dark that materials and circulation disappear.
- One-color black or yellow palette becomes flat, creepy, or tiring.
- Too many colored glass panels make the design gaudy.
- Sofa blocks the movement route and hides other facilities.
- Room is either an empty rectangle or a prop-packed narrow corridor.
- Multiple rooms repeat the same curved sofa, window, and warm-yellow treatment.
- Public hotel-lobby layout undermines privacy.
- Atmosphere is described only as “warm” without named light sources.

### Image Prompting And Editing

- Prompt mentions a reference image the user will not upload.
- Exact product text is paraphrased or becomes gibberish.
- Product is far too large in hand or on furniture.
- A local edit restyles the whole image.
- Removed object leaves a ghost, hole, blur, or broken surface.
- Prohibitions are split into an extra block despite the user's required single-prompt format.

### Brand Integration

- Ordinary shelves and metadata replace imagination.
- Floating data panels interrupt pacing.
- Product appears in an implausible usage context.
- Extra staff or customers violate the account's no-people convention.
- Final information card feels like a hard advertisement.
- Service is described but never changes the character's state.
- Soft-product pressing becomes a deep hand-shaped crater, which reads as collapse rather than comfort and support.
- A sleep-product demonstration shows only one pose despite a multi-posture support claim.

## Repair Rules

- Replace random spectacle with a visible cause-and-effect chain.
- Bring one threat close and keep the escape route clear.
- Anchor POV to a believable posture and piece of equipment.
- Delay the shelter reveal until a real occluder fills the frame.
- Reduce door and window scale to enlarge the shelter perceptually.
- Replace wall-to-wall glass with one protected view.
- Clear a walking corridor before adding décor.
- Add one spatial surprise—bridge, layered ceiling, garden, atrium, hidden service zone—rather than many props.
- Limit the palette and name real light sources.
- Convert brand claims into rooms, actions, and solved needs.
- End on relief and atmosphere rather than a sales message.
- For colossal near-field reveals, lock physical distance, frame occupancy, mandatory edge cropping, and foreground/background limits together.
- For first-person transport, move the character, carrier, and eye camera as one unit; show speed through parallax and local motion blur, not radial wind lines.
- Treat hands and feet as a limited narrative budget. Show them briefly for meaningful contact, not continuously to prove locomotion.
- For soft sleep products, separate shallow hand-pressure response from deeper body-support response, and make posture changes transfer pressure continuously across the same product.
