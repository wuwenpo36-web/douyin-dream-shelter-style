# Douyin Dream Shelter Style

Create work that feels native to this account: an immediate physical threat, a legible occupational identity, a magical but causally clear transformation, a gigantic themed shelter, and a strong emotional release from danger into safety.

## Default Account Grammar

Build most episodes around this sequence:

1. Establish a credible occupation or task in a visually strong environment.
2. Show two or three warning signs before the main disaster fully arrives.
3. Let the disaster directly break the task, route, vehicle, or support point.
4. Make the character react physically before using the transformation object.
5. Show the object being grasped and thrown, opened, pressed, or activated.
6. Let an object-specific material response fully occlude the frame.
7. Reveal a gigantic shelter only after the occlusion.
8. Give the character a plausible landing, boarding, or entry route.
9. Move through roughly three distinct, connected shelter spaces.
10. End on relief, ritual, warmth, or a meaningful object—not a hard sales card.

Treat this as a grammar, not a rigid template. Preserve continuity and motivation even when the user only asks for one image or one scene.

## Classify The Request First

Identify the requested artifact and answer at that altitude:

- **Idea list:** Offer genuinely different threats, transformation objects, and shelter forms. Explain the visual hook and why each pairing works.
- **Outline:** Write clear scene and action beats suitable for a client or prompt-writing agent. Do not bury the idea in poetic copy.
- **GPT Image prompt:** Return one polished, copyable English prompt. Integrate prohibitions into the same prompt. Do not split out a second negative-prompt section.
- **Image-edit prompt:** State exactly what changes, then lock everything else.
- **Video prompt or handoff brief:** Describe chronology, physical causality, POV lock, occlusion, reveal, landing, and continuity.
- **Interior plan:** Define a connected route, spatial purpose, distinctive composition, safety features, and atmosphere for each room.
- **Brand outline:** Make the service or product change the character's experience instead of merely appearing in frame.

If the user says an image is only an error example or will not be uploaded, never refer to it in the deliverable. Convert its useful lesson into a pure text-to-image description.

## Design The Hook For Immediate Readability

Prioritize one dominant near-field event over many small incidents. The opening should be understandable in under one second:

- a wave rising toward the lens;
- a cliff support tearing loose;
- a tree or rock mass entering the route;
- a road edge collapsing under the vehicle;
- a glass facade flexing under wind pressure;
- a heat-deformed surface failing under load;
- a burning object crossing the exact escape path.

Use the equivalent of “water hitting the face”: a large, fast, close foreground interaction that makes the viewer anticipate impact. Keep a readable direction of travel or escape so the frame is exciting rather than chaotic.

Distinguish an explicitly requested pre-disaster setup image from a retention-first video opening. For retention, begin at the **last safe second**: the body is already anchored to a failing route or vehicle, the threat is already in the near field, and contact is likely within roughly 0.5–1.5 seconds. Compress occupation, route, object source, and the causal background into the same opening frame instead of spending several seconds explaining them.

Use a two-peak opening rhythm when appropriate: physical impact and full-frame environmental occlusion -> brief reorientation -> visible object action -> object-specific second occlusion -> shelter reveal. Give the viewer a new irreversible state or unanswered physical question roughly every 0.8–1.5 seconds.

Only for an explicitly requested calm or pre-disaster first image, hold back the full catastrophe. Show two or three precursors such as an abnormal sky, crosswind, small debris, a warning light, surface cracks, rising water, smoke, or a strained support.

## Make The Disaster Causally Correct

The hazard must directly produce the failure. Do not substitute a random explosion for a disaster type.

- Extreme heat causes softened asphalt, rail or metal expansion, glass thermal cracking, electrical overheating, wildfire smoke, dehydration, and heat-stressed rockfall.
- Typhoon wind causes uplift, lateral debris, snapped supports, torn roofs, rolling objects, and storm surge.
- Mountain rain causes saturated soil, landslide, debris flow, treefall, road-edge collapse, runoff, and reduced visibility.
- Storm surge causes a visible advancing water mass, backflow, floating debris, loss of traction, and tunnel or street inundation.
- Snow and ice cause loss of friction, overloaded structures, whiteout, slab release, and brittle surfaces.

Show the cause before or with the failure. If the audience cannot tell why it happened, redesign the beat.

## Encode The Occupation Visually

Do not rely on captions to establish identity. Show the person actively working through:

- clothing and protective gear;
- tool use;
- vehicle controls;
- cargo or delivery packaging;
- route or work environment;
- a task already in progress.

The occupation should create the reason the character is exposed to danger. A courier is driving a loaded delivery tricycle on a mountain route; a lighthouse technician is repairing an exterior fitting; a climber is clipped into real anchors; a sailor is managing lines on a heeling deck.

## Preserve First-Person Physical Credibility

Use true eye-level POV unless the user requests otherwise. The lens must behave like a head or a clearly defined camera carrier.

- Show zero to two forearms or hands, and only the amount of torso or legs a real person would see in that posture.
- Keep limbs attached, correctly scaled, and anatomically plausible.
- Let horizon tilt, camera shake, motion blur, breath, balance corrections, and impacts follow the body.
- Let rain, spray, mud, fog, or condensation affect the lens only when the environment physically causes it.
- Preserve droplets or smears for a plausible duration instead of resetting the lens instantly.
- Use real occluders—water sheets, smoke, cloth, wall edges, darkness, doors—to hide difficult transitions.

When the user wants a rapid scene traversal without first-person walking or running, define a camera carrier such as a drone-like cinematic glide, cable rig, flying vehicle, or accelerated visual pull. Do not invent visible human locomotion.

## Build A Legible Transformation

The transformation object should feel inevitable in retrospect. Match it to the occupation, setting, theme, or emotional promise.

Use this causal chain:

1. The object is already visible or is clearly removed from a pocket, bag, vehicle, or workstation.
2. The hand grips it with a plausible action.
3. It is thrown, opened, pressed, broken, or placed.
4. It contacts a surface, airflow, water, or another object.
5. A material response belonging to that object expands through the frame.
6. The response fully or mostly occludes the image.
7. The shelter appears only after the occlusion clears.

Use material-specific transformation media: pinecone dust or brown spore-like mist, bottle condensation and pale-blue vapor, chocolate ribbons and warm cocoa mist, mango-gold vapor, paper fibers and ink particles, crystalline frost, or fabric-like silk flow. Avoid generic neon portals unless the concept specifically calls for one.

Never reveal the finished shelter before the object action. Do not let the object disappear, duplicate, or transform off-screen without cause.

## Design The Exterior As A Real Shelter

Make the shelter visibly large enough to contain several rooms. Establish scale with:

- a deliberately small human-scale door;
- repeated window bays or floor levels;
- railings, steps, lamps, and furniture at normal scale;
- trees, vehicles, or terrain when the scene allows them.

Communicate safety through mass and detailing: rounded but robust volumes, layered shells, deep eaves, sealed openings, drainage, protected access, structural ribs, weather-resistant materials, and maintained surfaces.

Keep themed architecture sophisticated. Translate the source object into silhouette, section, material, roof geometry, circulation, and a few unmistakable details. Do not simply enlarge the object into a cartoon toy.

Keep premium structures clean, precise, and cared for. “High-end” does not mean broken boards, random rust, or distressed surfaces. For pure-white asset sheets, use a genuinely seamless white field with only a soft contact shadow.

## Plan Interiors As A Safety Journey

Use a connected route rather than three unrelated rooms:

1. **Arrival and buffer:** drying, washing, equipment storage, decontamination, changing, charging, or a protected entry ritual.
2. **Signature experience:** the most memorable spatial idea—rain garden, ecological valley, library hearth, music room, protected water court, observatory, greenhouse, or game room.
3. **Private recovery:** bedroom, bath, quiet lounge, media room, or sleep pod with privacy and useful facilities.

Give each space a different composition, ceiling language, focal point, palette balance, and emotional purpose. Keep a clear walkable path through the frame. Never let an oversized sofa block the route or hide the rest of the room.

Make safety visible: small or moderate laminated windows, deep reveals, storm shutters, drainage, handrails, emergency storage, charging, drying, food and water, medical supplies, and controlled access. Large glass walls usually weaken shelter psychology unless they are deeply protected and structurally explained.

Create warmth with physically sourced light, layered textiles, human-scale furniture, readable shadow detail, and one or two contrasting accent colors. Avoid both extremes: an empty rectangular showroom and a cramped prop-filled room.

## Create Atmosphere Without Making It Unreadable

Describe the whole room, not only the view outside. Define:

- the exterior weather and color temperature;
- the main practical light sources;
- one secondary or accent source;
- the materials receiving the light;
- where the cool exterior reflection stops;
- the quiet activity or recovery ritual in the room.

Use warm residential light around 2700–3000K as the main source. Keep night interiors dim enough to feel atmospheric but bright enough to read furniture, circulation, and faces or hands. Use RGB or neon as a controlled accent, never as the only illumination.

Prefer one main color plus two secondary colors, or two main colors plus one accent. Avoid monochrome black, monochrome yellow, or a ceiling filled with many unrelated colored glass panels.

## Integrate Brands As Experience

Treat branded content as part of the shelter's function:

- begin from a credible pain point;
- place the product or service in a context where it naturally belongs;
- show the character using it;
- show an immediate sensory, spatial, or practical response;
- return to the story without pausing for a sales explanation.

Turn abstract claims into spaces, actions, and objects. Source quality can become an indoor ecological valley; service quality can become a protected rain court; softness can become a physical landing or reclining experience; air purification can become visible intake and clean-air circulation with values changing naturally.

Use robots, mascots, moving baskets, mechanical arms, doors, lighting, sound, and self-operating facilities when the account should remain free of extra human characters.

Keep exact claims, dates, product text, packaging, logos, and numeric values faithful to approved reference material. Do not invent medical, safety, origin, membership, or quality claims.

## GPT Image Prompt Workflow

For every GPT Image request:

1. Decide whether it is pure text-to-image, image edit, or reference-guided generation.
2. Assign each uploaded image one role: base scene, object construction, scale, product detail, mood only, or error example.
3. Lock camera height, POV, orientation, frame ratio, anatomy, object placement, scale, materials, lighting sources, and weather.
4. Describe the most important relationship first: character-to-threat, character-to-object, shelter-to-landscape, or furniture-to-route.
5. Put exact requested text in quotation marks and require it to be fully legible.
6. End the same prompt with integrated prohibitions. Do not create a separate negative-prompt block for this user's workflow.
7. Return only the prompt when the user says “直接给我 prompt”.

## Final Quality Gate

Before answering, verify all of the following:

- The first frame reads immediately and has one dominant visual event.
- The hazard actually causes the failure.
- The occupation is visible without explanation.
- The POV and anatomy are physically plausible.
- The object action and transformation are causally continuous.
- The shelter does not appear early.
- The exterior reads as gigantic, safe, clean, and inhabitable.
- The entry or landing is physically possible.
- The three interior spaces are connected but visually distinct.
- Walking routes remain clear.
- Lighting comes from named physical sources.
- Warmth and safety clearly contrast with the exterior threat.
- Brand use changes the experience instead of stopping the story.
- Exact text and product scale are preserved.
- The output matches the requested artifact and contains no unnecessary explanation.
